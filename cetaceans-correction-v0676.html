What was corrected

The previous pass relied on thin shared body shapes, long conical tail tapers and a uniform shiny surface. This correction changes the geometry and material response together. Forehead and rostrum transitions, underside jaw lines, pectoral roots, fin planforms, respiratory slits and horizontal flukes are revised. Rounded-flipper species retain broader tips; the long-finned pilot’s flipper proportion is kept within the FAO reference range.

Body pigment maps are 4096 × 2048. Each profile also has its own 2048 × 1024 body relief and roughness maps plus separate appendage maps. Scar groups, oval marks and pigmentation follow species context rather than one repeated scratch pattern. Fine relief is restrained for smooth cetacean skin. A low-amplitude generated microdetail layer varies the wet-film roughness; its full prompt and built-in imagegen provenance are recorded. It supplies no species markings or scientific evidence. Inspection lighting illuminates the underside, and the compatibility renderer uses higher resolution for paused close-ups.

Species-by-species correction

Pygmy right whale

Caperea marginata

Fuller fusiform trunk, moderately arched mouth and small rounded flippers. Retained two shallow throat creases, small posterior fin, and no right-whale callosities.

Feeding and physiological context

Baleen filters small plankton, including copepods and krill documented in stomach contents. Detailed live feeding mechanics remain poorly observed; a right-whale-like feeding animation would overstate the evidence.

Sei whale

Balaenoptera borealis

Deeper thorax and shorter taper, narrow arched rostrum with one principal ridge, tall hooked posterior fin, 42 short ventral grooves and sparse irregular oval scars.

Feeding and physiological context

Baleen retains small prey after water is filtered. Sei whales use both skim feeding and engulfment, with copepods, krill and small schooling fish in their diet. Their ventral grooves are relatively short compared with many rorquals.

Common minke whale

Balaenoptera acutorostrata

Pointed rostrum, fuller chest, modelled shoulder contour, discrete white pectoral bands and a pale ventrum with lateral shoulder shading. Retained 56 ventral grooves.

Feeding and physiological context

Engulfment of schooling fish and small crustaceans uses a distensible ventral pouch followed by baleen filtration. This northern phenotype has a white mid-flipper band; the band is not a feeding organ or universal to every common-minke form.

Antarctic minke whale

Balaenoptera bonaerensis

Fuller Antarctic-minke body, pointed rostrum, darker unbanded pectorals, pale underside and 30 ventral grooves. This is a separate phenotype from the northern common minke.

Feeding and physiological context

Engulfment and baleen filtration capture Antarctic krill and other prey. The selected 30-groove field is within NOAA’s 22–38 range. This animal lacks the northern common-minke mid-flipper band; baleen colour can be asymmetric.

Rice's whale

Balaenoptera ricei

Fuller trunk and caudal peduncle, three rostral ridges, bilateral pale ventrum and revised hooked fin. No false Omura-type jaw asymmetry.

Feeding and physiological context

Rice’s whales are baleen filter feeders. Limited tag records indicate deep daytime foraging near the seafloor and shallower nighttime occupancy. These observations do not justify a universal surface-lunge trajectory or a species-wide dive schedule.

Bryde’s / Eden’s whale

Balaenoptera edeni

Fuller Bryde/Eden body and revised fins, three rostral ridges and 48 ventral grooves. Both existing display profiles remain; neither authored shape diagnoses the contested species split.

Feeding and physiological context

Bryde/Eden whales filter small schooling fish and crustaceans through baleen after taking in prey and water. The archive retains two display forms under one disputed taxon concept; morphology and this single mitochondrial record do not resolve the proposed split.

Omura's whale

Balaenoptera omurai

One rostral ridge, right pale lower jaw and blaze, asymmetric flank chevrons and pale pectoral leading edges. Revised thorax, hooked fin and 86 long ventral grooves.

Feeding and physiological context

Observed Madagascar animals performed lunge feeding. Small crustaceans were found in the study area, but complete prey composition was unresolved. The asymmetric jaw, blaze and chevrons are identification features; a pale jaw does not imply a different filtration mechanism.

Long-finned pilot whale

Globicephala melas

Rounded adult melon, robust trunk, deepened caudal peduncle, broad anterior dorsal fin and long swept flippers. Selected ventral anchor and subtle saddle remain individual artwork.

Feeding and physiological context

Long-finned pilot whales hunt squid and also consume fish. Echolocation supports prey finding in dark water. Teeth assist prey capture, while suction can contribute to ingestion; no generic rorqual pouch or baleen belongs on this odontocete.

Risso's dolphin

Grampus griseus

Rounded beakless melon with median crease, robust thorax, narrow tailstock, tall falcate dorsal fin and tapered pectorals. Pale head and grouped, curved rake scars replace uniform random scratches.

Feeding and physiological context

Risso’s dolphins primarily take squid and other cephalopods. The sparse anterior mandibular teeth, usually two to seven pairs with no upper teeth, differ from a dolphin’s full conical tooth rows. Some studied adults initiated deep foraging dives with a right-sided roll.

Amazon river dolphin

Inia geoffrensis

Long narrow rostrum with revised jawline, fuller flexible trunk, broad rounded flippers, small eyes and low long dorsal ridge. Selected gray-pink mottling is not a universal adult colour.

Feeding and physiological context

Botos capture a variety of fish with a long rostrum. Anterior teeth are conical, while posterior teeth have flanges that assist processing prey. Mobile cervical anatomy and broad flippers aid manoeuvring; pinkness varies with age, sex and individual history.

Spinner dolphin

Stenella longirostris

Long beak and smoothly rising melon, fuller but slender trunk, upright dorsal fin, swept pectorals and a three-part Gray’s spinner pigment pattern. No fish-like lateral tail sweep.

Feeding and physiological context

Spinner dolphins feed mainly at night on mesopelagic fish, squid and shrimp. In Hawaiian populations, daytime resting and nighttime offshore feeding are well documented; that local routine is not assumed for every population. Aerial spins are a separate behaviour from underwater propulsion.

Striped dolphin

Stenella coeruleoalba

Rounded melon, moderate beak, fuller trunk, curved dorsal fin, lateral blaze and distinct eye-to-flipper and eye-to-anal pigment branches. Stripes are separate from the mouth crease.

Feeding and physiological context

Striped dolphins feed on fish and cephalopods, with prey and dive patterns varying geographically. Echolocation helps detect prey. Their characteristic eye-to-flipper and eye-to-anal stripes are pigment paths, not anatomical seams or grooves.

Irrawaddy dolphin

Orcaella brevirostris

Rounded beakless forehead, short mouthline, fuller trunk, broad rounded flippers, small posterior fin and a forward-opening U-shaped blowhole slit. No Australian snubfin range is inferred.

Feeding and physiological context

Irrawaddy dolphins consume fish and other prey in coastal, estuarine and some river habitats. Broad flippers support manoeuvring and the rounded head lacks a projecting beak. This archive record excludes the separately recognised Australian snubfin dolphin.

Northern bottlenose whale

Hyperoodon ampullatus

Rounded overhanging forehead of the selected mature male, short bottle-shaped rostrum, robust trunk, small posterior fin and short pectorals. Apical teeth are hidden in this closed-mouth model.

Feeding and physiological context

Northern bottlenose whales are deep-diving odontocetes that primarily take squid, with other prey also recorded. Suction feeding suits the short rostrum and reduced dentition. The apical teeth of adult males are generally hidden with the mouth closed.

Blainville's beaked whale

Mesoplodon densirostris

Low sloping melon, raised male mandibular arch, forward-angled erupted teeth behind the tip, fuller midbody and small posterior fin. Body marks remain selected individual artwork.

Feeding and physiological context

Blainville’s beaked whales forage at depth on squid and fish. Reduced dentition and suction feeding differ from a dolphin’s tooth-lined grasping jaws. The selected mature male has erupted teeth on the raised lower jaw behind the tip; females and juveniles differ.

Dwarf sperm whale

Kogia sima

Compact, deep body with a sloping blunt head, short underslung mouth, small flippers and a relatively tall central dorsal fin. The false-gill crescent follows the eye region and is only pigment.

Feeding and physiological context

Dwarf sperm whales take squid and other prey using suction-assisted feeding. Their underslung lower jaw bears most teeth. The pale false-gill crescent is skin pigment; the animal breathes air through a blowhole and has no gills. Defensive reddish fluid is released from the intestine.

The physiology cards now explain feeding mechanisms, respiration, locomotion and skin separately. Baleen filtration, suction-assisted odontocete feeding, reduced beaked-whale dentition and the false-gill marking of Kogia are distinguished. Northern bottlenose male teeth remain concealed in the closed mouth; the selected adult male Blainville retains its exposed teeth behind the beak tip. Feeding controls stay unavailable where the jaw, prey and internal mechanism are unfinished.

Evidence and review status

External form was compared with NOAA photographs and species accounts, FAO identification drawings and Omura field observations. FAO’s historical taxonomy and conservation assessments are not substituted for modern species treatments. Age, sex, population and individual markings remain important: the selected male beaked whales and mature Risso’s are examples rather than species-wide averages.

The reconstruction remains original artwork. No measured scan, calibrated skin reflectance, solved hydrodynamics, gas exchange or oxygen-budget simulation is claimed. Independent specialist validation is still pending. Software tests check data integrity, mesh behaviour, texture routing and regressions; they do not certify photorealism or biological accuracy.

The deposited molecular records are unchanged. They are evidence from separately identified sequence records, not a claimed genotype for the rendered animals.
